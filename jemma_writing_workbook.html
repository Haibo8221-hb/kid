🌡 Warm-Up Drills 5 min each

These are quick, low-pressure exercises to get words flowing. Do one before a longer writing session — like stretching before a run.

Five Senses in Five Minutes

★ Easy

Pick an object near you right now — a lamp, a mug, a window, anything. Write exactly one sentence for each sense that describes it. The catch: you cannot name the object. Let the reader figure it out from the sensory clues.

Sight: _______________________________________________
Sound: _______________________________________________
Smell: _______________________________________________
Touch: _______________________________________________
Taste: (imaginative — what would it taste like?) _________
If you're stuck on 'taste', think metaphorically. A dusty old book might taste "of forgotten afternoons."
2

The Sentence Stretch

★ Easy

Start with a bare 3-word sentence. Then rewrite it five times, adding one new detail each time without changing the core meaning.

Start: "The bottle arrived."

Version 1 (add colour): ________________________________
Version 2 (add texture/surface): ______________________
Version 3 (add sound): _______________________________
Version 4 (add emotion/atmosphere): _________________
Version 5 (add a simile or metaphor): ________________

Self-Check

  • Does each version add genuine new information (not just more words)?
  • Is Version 5 still one sentence?
  • Could a reader picture the scene differently after each version?

Mood Flip

★ Easy

Threatening Version

The Outsider's Diary

★★ Medium

Jemma, your story's narrator is an exile — this is your strength. Write a diary entry (200 words) from the perspective of someone who doesn't belong. Choose one:

  • A mermaid who has just grown legs and is walking on land for the first time
  • A ghost watching their own family eat dinner without them
  • The last person on Earth who remembers how to read
  • A dragon disguised as a human, attending a normal school

Must Include

  • At least two sensory details that show their difference (e.g. the mermaid notices gravity pressing on her new bones)
  • One moment of longing for their original world
  • One moment of unexpected joy in the new world
  • A closing line that leaves the reader thinking

6

The Unreliable Narrator

★★★ Challenging

Write 250 words where the narrator is lying to themselves about something obvious to the reader. The reader should slowly realise the truth while the narrator keeps pretending.

Choose one:
• A character insists they're "fine" after being left behind by their best friend
• A character claims the strange sounds in their house are "just the pipes"
• A character says they "don't care" about the message in the bottle — while checking the horizon every day
This is an advanced GCSE-level skill. The key is what the narrator doesn't say — the gaps between their words. Read the first page of The Remains of the Day by Kazuo Ishiguro for a masterclass in this.

The Empty Room

★★ Medium

Describe an empty room in such detail that the reader can deduce who lived there and what happened to them — without you ever stating it directly.

Choose one scenario (don't tell the reader which):
• A child who was taken away suddenly
• An old person who died peacefully after a long life
• Someone who packed in a hurry and fled
• A scientist whose experiment went wrong

Clue Ideas

  • What's still there? What's missing?
  • What's in the bin? What's on the walls?
  • What does the dust tell you? Where is it thick and where is it disturbed?
  • What's the one object that doesn't belong?

The Bottle: Part Two

★★ Medium

Continue your original story. She has read the message: "Sail across the water, my love. And find your kind."

Write 300-400 words covering what happens next. Does she build a boat? Does she discover she's not the only exile? Is there another message?

Must Include

  • At least one sensory callback to your original story (pale blue skin, white hair, the glass bottle)
  • A decision moment — the point where she chooses to act
  • An ending that raises a new question for a potential Part Three
12

Circular Narrative

★★★ Challenging

Write a 300-word story where the first and last sentence are identical — but the reader understands them completely differently the second time because of everything that happened in between.

Possible opening/closing sentences:
• "The sea was exactly where she'd left it."
• "Some messages are better left unread."
• "She had always known she was different."
This is a sophisticated GCSE-grade structural technique. The meaning of the sentence doesn't change — the reader's understanding does. Think about what information you reveal in the middle that re-contextualises the opening.
🖌 Language Craft 10-15 min

Kill Your Darlings: The "Get" Hunt

★ Easy

"Get" is the most overused verb in student writing. Take a piece of your own writing (or the paragraph below) and replace every instance of "get/got/gotten" with a more precise verb.

Before
She got to the shore and got the bottle out of the water. She got the cork out and got hold of the paper. Then she got a strange feeling.
Possible Revision
She waded to the shore and plucked the bottle from the water. She worked the cork free and pinched the edge of the paper. Then a strange feeling crept over her.

17

Pacing: The Slow-Motion Moment

★★★ Challenging

Take a moment that would normally take one second in real time and stretch it across 150 words. This is called a "slow-motion moment" and it's a powerful GCSE narrative technique.

Choose your one-second moment:
• The cork coming out of the bottle
• A character's foot crossing a threshold
• The moment before diving into water
• A door beginning to open
How to stretch time: describe every micro-sensation, every flicker of thought, every detail the character notices in that split second. Use short sentences for quick observations, longer ones for the thoughts that unspool.
18

The Constraint Challenge

★★★ Challenging

Write a 150-word scene with strict constraints. Constraints force creativity — professional writers use this technique.

Scene: A character discovers a hidden door.

Constraints:
• No adverbs ending in -ly
• No form of the verb "to be" (is, was, were, are, am, been, being)
• Must include one colour and one sound
Removing "to be" verbs forces you into active, vivid sentences. Instead of "The door was old", you write "The door sagged on its hinges" or "Centuries had warped the oak."
📖 The PEEZL Framework Foundation

How to Write a GCSE Analysis Paragraph

Analysis paragraphs are the core skill for GCSE English Literature and Language. They ask you to explain how a writer creates an effect — not just what happens. The PEEZL structure gives you a reliable formula.

P — Point

One clear sentence stating your argument about the text. What effect does the writer create?

"Orwell presents the room as oppressive and claustrophobic to reflect Winston's lack of freedom."

E — Evidence

A short, embedded quotation that supports your point. Embed means weave it into your sentence — don't just drop it in.

The description of the "vile wind" and "stripped" walls creates a sense of...

E — Explain (Technique)

Name the writer's technique and explain what it does. Always name a technique.

The adjective "vile" personifies the wind, suggesting that even nature has turned hostile...

Z — Zoom (Word Level)

Zoom in on one specific word and analyse its connotations. This is where top marks live.

The word "stripped" carries connotations of violence and exposure — the room has been forcibly robbed of comfort...

L — Link

Connect back to the wider text, the writer's purpose, or the reader's response. Why does this matter?

This creates a powerful sense of isolation that mirrors Winston's psychological state throughout the novel, making the reader feel the weight of the Party's control.

Quick Technique Glossary

TechniqueDefinitionExample from Published Texts
SimileComparison using "like" or "as""It was as if the disease had sharpened my senses" (Poe)
MetaphorDirect comparison (says it IS the thing)"the distant savage lair...was the sea" — sea as a wild beast's den (Dickens)
PersonificationGiving human qualities to non-human things"the wind was rushing" — wind given deliberate, aggressive movement (Dickens)
Sensory imageryDescription that appeals to the 5 senses"warm and clean" room, "fresh ice cubes", "two tall glasses" (Dahl)
Pathetic fallacyWeather/setting reflecting emotion"dark flat wilderness", "low leaden line" — gloomy setting mirrors Pip's isolation (Dickens)
Short sentenceA brief sentence for emphasis or shock"How, then, am I mad?" (Poe) — abrupt challenge to the reader
AdjectiveA describing word"dreadfully nervous", "dark flat wilderness", "warm and clean"
Verb choiceSpecific action words that create effect"sharpened" (Poe) — senses as blades; "rushing" (Dickens) — wind as attacker
JuxtapositionPlacing two contrasting things side by sideCosy domestic scene → sudden murder (Dahl); "I heard...heaven...hell" (Poe)
First-person narration"I" voice — limited to one character's perspective"True! — nervous — very, very dreadfully nervous I had been" (Poe)
ForeshadowingHinting at what will happen later"the empty chair opposite" — hints at the husband's absence to come (Dahl)
SymbolismAn object representing a bigger ideaThe Thermos bucket with "fresh ice cubes" — preserved, cold domestic perfection (Dahl)
