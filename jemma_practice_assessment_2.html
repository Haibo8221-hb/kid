Section B — Reading [29 marks]

Spend around 35 minutes on this section. Read the passage carefully. You may refer back to it when answering the questions that follow.

This extract is from the opening of Treasure Island by Robert Louis Stevenson (1883). The narrator, Jim Hawkins, is a young boy whose family runs a small inn by the sea. One day, a mysterious stranger arrives...

I remember him as if it were yesterday, as he came plodding to the inn door, his sea-chest following behind him in a hand-barrow — a tall, strong, heavy, nut-brown man, his tarry pigtail falling over the shoulder of his soiled blue coat, his hands ragged and scarred, with black, broken nails; and the sabre cut across one cheek, a dirty, livid white.

I remember him looking round the cove and whistling to himself as he did so, and then breaking out in that old sea-song that he sang so often afterwards:

"Fifteen men on the dead man's chest —
Yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!"

in the high, old tottering voice that seemed to have been tuned and broken at the capstan bars. Then he rapped on the door with a bit of stick like a handspike that he carried, and when my father appeared, called roughly for a glass of rum. This, when it was brought to him, he drank slowly, like a connoisseur, lingering on the taste and still looking about him at the cliffs and up at our signboard.

"This is a handy cove," says he at length; "and a pleasant sittypated grog-shop. Much company, mate?"

My father told him no, very little company, the more was the pity.

"Well, then," said he, "this is the berth for me. Here you, matey," he cried to the man who trundled the barrow; "bring up alongside and help up my chest. I'll stay here a bit," he continued. "I'm a plain man; rum and bacon and eggs is what I want, and that head up there for to watch ships off. What you mought call me? You mought call me captain. Oh, I see what you're at — there"; and he threw down three or four gold pieces on the threshold. "You can tell me when I've worked through that," says he, looking as fierce as a commander.

And indeed bad as his clothes were and coarsely as he spoke, he had none of the appearance of a man who sailed before the mast, but seemed like a mate or skipper accustomed to be obeyed or to strike. The man who came with the barrow told us the mail had set him down the morning before at the Royal George, that he had inquired what inns there were along the coast, and hearing ours well spoken of, I suppose, and described as lonely, had chosen it from the others for his place of residence. And that was all we could learn of our guest.

Glossary

tarry covered with tar (a black sticky substance used on ships)
livid dark bluish-grey in colour
capstan a machine on a ship used for lifting heavy objects like the anchor
connoisseur an expert who knows and appreciates fine quality
sittypated a slang word meaning "situated" (the captain speaks in an uneducated way)
berth a place to sleep on a ship; here used to mean a place to stay
threshold the entrance or doorway to a building

Section B — Reading [29 marks]

Q1. Three details [3 marks] — 1 mark each. Accept any three of:

  • "ragged and scarred" hands / "black, broken nails"
  • "sabre cut across one cheek, a dirty, livid white"
  • "soiled blue coat"
  • "nut-brown" skin suggesting a life outdoors / at sea
  • "fierce as a commander" expression
  • "tall, strong, heavy" build

Q2. Contradiction [3 marks]

  • 1 mark: Identifies that he claims to be ordinary but acts like someone important/dangerous
  • 1 mark: Evidence — "throws down three or four gold pieces" shows wealth; "fierce as a commander" shows authority
  • 1 mark: Explains what this reveals — he is hiding his true identity; he is used to power; he is not what he pretends to be

Q3. Final paragraph analysis [3 marks]

  • 1 mark: Identifies the contrast — "bad as his clothes were" vs "seemed like a mate or skipper"
  • 1 mark: Evidence — "accustomed to be obeyed or to strike" suggests violence and authority
  • 1 mark: Explains — Stevenson reveals the captain is more dangerous than he first appears; the phrase "or to strike" hints at cruelty

Q4. Summary [4 marks]

  • 2 marks: Includes all three key points — WHO (a scarred, rough sea captain), WHERE (the Admiral Benbow inn / a lonely coastal inn), WHAT (arrives, demands rum, pays in gold, decides to stay)
  • 1 mark: Stays within ~60 words and uses own wording (no direct copying)
  • 1 mark: Clear, concise, grammatically correct sentences
  • Model answer: "A rough, scarred old sailor arrives at a lonely seaside inn carrying a heavy sea chest. He sings a grim sea shanty, demands rum, and speaks roughly to the innkeeper. Though dressed in dirty clothes, he throws down gold coins and speaks with the authority of a commander. He decides to stay, having deliberately chosen this remote location."

Q5. PEZL Analysis Paragraph [6 marks]

  • Point [1]: Clear argument about how Stevenson presents the captain as dangerous
  • Evidence [1]: At least one embedded quotation from paragraphs 1 or 6
  • Zoom [2]: Analyses one specific word and its connotations in depth (e.g. "livid" → bruised/dead colour, hints at violence; "accustomed to strike" → casual violence as habit; "sabre cut" → weapon wound, combat history)
  • Link [1]: Connects to reader's response — makes us fear for Jim and his family
  • SPAG [1]: Accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar

Q6. Vocabulary [2 marks] — ½ mark each

  • A. plodding = trudging
  • B. tottering = shaky
  • C. inquired = asked
  • D. residence = home

Q7. Extended Analysis [8 marks]

Two PEEZL paragraphs required. Award:

  • Paragraph 1 — Appearance [up to 3]: Analyses physical description. Should zoom on words like "sabre cut" (weapon wound), "livid" (death-like colour), "ragged and scarred", "black broken nails", "tarry pigtail". Must name techniques (adjective choice, sensory imagery).
  • Paragraph 2 — Speech & Behaviour [up to 3]: Analyses his actions and words. Should zoom on verbs like "rapped", "called roughly", "looking as fierce as a commander"; should note the contradiction of "I'm a plain man" with his commanding behaviour; should analyse the sea shanty ("Fifteen men on the dead man's chest") as foreshadowing death.
  • Overall quality [up to 2]: Both paragraphs embedded with quotations; techniques named accurately; zoom goes beyond surface meaning; links connect to overall impression of menace; SPAG is secure.
